METHODS

How the risk scores work

Using only what was known on January 1 of a year, we estimate whether a SNAP-authorized grocery store will stop accepting SNAP within the next 12 months. The model learns from every grocery store in North Carolina; this site focuses on Mecklenburg County.

From records to a score

01

Data

USDA's historical SNAP retailer records, 2005–2025: each store's type, location and authorization dates, joined to Census neighborhood data.

02

Model

Trained on 2008–2018, tuned on 2019–2021, and tested once on 2022–2024, so it's always judged on years it hasn't seen.

03

Score

Each store gets a 0–100% chance of exiting, grouped into Low, Medium and High with cutoffs set on the 2019–2021 years.

What counts as an exit

Exit

The store's SNAP authorization ends, and no new authorization appears at the same address within 90 days.

Not an exit: ownership change

A new authorization at the same address within 90 days. [Share] of grocery authorizations that ended through 2021 were ownership changes.

An exit isn't always a closure. A store can also withdraw from SNAP or be disqualified. For a household that uses SNAP, the result is the same: they can no longer use their benefits there.

Limits and fairness

EFFECTIVE NOV 4, 2026

New USDA stocking rules

Every SNAP store must now carry 7 varieties in each of 4 staple groups: dairy, fruits and vegetables, grains, and protein. Stores that can't comply lose authorization. Our training years predate the rule, so scores don't reflect it. Stores that struggle to stock the required variety may be at greater risk than shown.

Patterns, not causes

Neighborhood data can lead the model to flag stores in Black or low-income areas because of correlation, not anything about the store. We compare error rates across income and demographic groups and report them with sample sizes. See the error breakdown

Missing history

Stores that left SNAP before 2005 aren't in USDA's file, so the model learns only from stores that had already lasted that long.

Distance is simplified

"Within 1 mile" is straight-line distance. It ignores highways, bus routes, and whether the nearest store is a full-service grocery.

What the data can't see

Sales, rent, leases and owners' plans drive most exits, and none of it is public. Treat a score as a prompt to check in, not a forecast of a decision.
