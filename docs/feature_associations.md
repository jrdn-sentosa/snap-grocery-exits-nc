Store type is by far the strongest single association (small grocery stores exit at 15.8% against 1.6% for supermarkets, a 9.7x ratio), followed by `chain_size` (stores sharing a name with 497 or more NC retailers exit at 0.7%, against 7.9% for names shared by 12 or fewer; the ratio is 0.08), `store_age_years` (newest quintile 9.2%, oldest 2.5%) and the tract measures of disadvantage (`pct_poverty`, `pct_snap_hh`, `pct_no_vehicle`, `unemployment_rate`, low `median_hh_income`: roughly 1.8x to 2.5x between the extreme quintiles), plus `n_convenience_1mi` (2.2x). Holding store type fixed shrinks most of these: the tract poverty and SNAP-household ratios fall to 0.94 and 1.14 among small and medium grocery stores (they stay at 1.64 and 1.54 among supermarkets, super stores and large groceries, where exits are rare and each quintile has only a few dozen exits), `store_age_years` weakens from 0.27 to 0.65 and 0.77, and `chain_size` reverses sign among small and medium stores (1.70) while keeping its direction among large formats (0.31), so its overall association largely reflects that chains are mostly large formats. Only `n_convenience_1mi` (1.30 and 1.91) and, weakly, `pct_snap_hh` (1.14 and 1.54) stay above 1 in both groups. `pop_density_sqmi` has no association (1.01). These are univariate associations on training rows only (2012-2018), not causal effects and not model results: features are correlated with each other and with store type, group sizes in the stratified columns are small, and `unemployment_rate` is missing for the whole 2012 cohort.
